# BHS Has No Audience Data — What That Actually Means

---

## The blunt version

**You cannot sell sponsorship without audience numbers.** A sponsor's first question is "how many people, and who are they?" No deck design fixes a missing answer.

So the reason BHS has no sponsors isn't a missing deck. It's missing infrastructure.

**Which is the more valuable product.** "I'll design your sponsorship deck" is a $400 Fiverr job. "I'll build the system that makes you sellable, then build the deck" is a real engagement — and almost nobody offers it, because it's less glamorous than design.

You just found your actual service by hitting the wall.

---

## Two things to do at once

### 1. Sell one event, not the organisation

You don't need historical member data to sell sponsorship for a **specific upcoming event**. Sponsors routinely buy against *projected* attendance — that's normal practice, not a workaround.

Pick BHS's biggest event this semester. Build the kit around that one thing:

- Projected attendance, with the basis stated honestly ("last year's turnout," "room capacity," "comparable events")
- Who attends — you know this qualitatively even without a spreadsheet
- What a sponsor gets: signage, speaking moment, table presence, a mention in materials
- Two or three tiers, small — $500 / $1,000 / $2,500

That's sellable in September and it's a complete portfolio piece.

### 2. Start counting immediately

Set this up before the first event of the semester. It takes an afternoon and costs nothing.

| Tool | Job |
|---|---|
| **Google Form + QR code** | Event sign-in. Name, email, year, major. Print the QR on a table tent. |
| **Google Sheet** | Auto-populates from the form. That's your member list, built from scratch. |
| **Instagram insights** | Follower count and reach already exist — pull them now |
| **Simple headcount** | Someone clicks a counter at the door. Compare to sign-ins to estimate capture rate. |

Three events between September and November gives you real attendance data, a genuine email list, and demographics. **That's enough for a full org-wide kit by December and a spring sponsorship push.**

Add one line to the sign-in form: *"Can we share your name and major with employers who partner with BHS?"* — a yes/no box. That opt-in is what makes an employer résumé book legitimate later.

## What this does for BHS beyond sponsorship

You're Secretary of an organisation with no membership records. Building that system is *your actual job*, and it's the kind of thing that outlasts you — the next Secretary inherits a functioning list instead of starting over.

That's also a better résumé line than the sponsorship kit: *"Built the membership tracking and engagement system for a 100+ member organisation, enabling its first corporate sponsorship programme."* That's operations and infrastructure, not a design project.

---

## The lesson for the business

Add one more filter, and it's cheap:

> **Does this organisation know how many people it reaches?**

If yes → they can buy a kit today.
If no → they need the system first, which is a bigger job, a longer sale, and usually a client with less money.

That splits your market cleanly:

| | Ready to buy a kit | Needs the system first |
|---|---|---|
| **Who** | Established festivals, conferences, businesses with analytics | Student orgs, volunteer-run events, young nonprofits |
| **Sale** | Faster | Slower |
| **Money** | Has budget | Usually doesn't |
| **Risk** | May already have materials | May never be able to pay |

**Target the first group for revenue.** Syracuse International Film Festival, Festival of Music, and the established Syracuse festivals all have attendance figures — they've been running for years.

**Use the second group for case studies**, which is exactly what BHS is. It's free, it's yours, and it produces the proof you need to charge the first group.

That was always the right role for BHS. The missing data just makes it explicit.
