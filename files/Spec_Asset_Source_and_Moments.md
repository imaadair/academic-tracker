## On AI clipping tools

Short answer: **use AI for the mechanical parts, never for the selection.**

### The tools that exist

| Tool | What it does | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **OpusClip** | Auto-finds "viral" moments, reframes, captions. Most hands-off. | Free tier / ~$29 mo |
| **Vizard** | Highlight detection, vertical reframe, captions, brand templates | Free tier / ~$30 mo |
| **Choppity** | Clip detection plus transcript-native editing and scheduling | Free tier / ~$30 mo |
| **CapCut** | Manual but fast; good auto-captions, free | Free |
| **Descript** | Text-based editing — edit video by deleting words | Free tier / ~$24 mo |

### Why you shouldn't hand them the selection

Your entire pitch to Ben is *"I know which forty seconds of a panel matters."* That's editorial judgment — the same muscle as script coverage, and the reason this work belongs on a development résumé at all.

An AI virality scorer does that part for $29 a month. If you outsource it, you're selling something Ben could buy himself, and the thing that makes you worth $2,500 disappears.

There's a practical problem too. AI-clipper output has a recognizable house style — auto-zoom punch-ins, bouncing karaoke captions, that particular rhythm. Film festival audiences have taste. Looking like every other AI-generated podcast clip is an actual liability with this client.

### The stack worth using

| Job | Tool | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Transcription | Final Cut's Transcribe to Captions, or MacWhisper | Free, and you already own it |
| **Selection** | **You, reading the transcript** | This is the product |
| Cut, caption, brand | Final Cut Pro | Full control, matches the spec, no licensing question |
| Optional speed-up later | CapCut or Descript | Test once the retainer exists |

**For the spec asset, cut it by hand.** It's the single piece of work that has to be excellent — it's the entire reason Ben replies. Once you're delivering 16 assets a month and your time is the constraint, revisit the tools then.

## If the footage isn't usable

I picked that video from its transcript. I couldn't get the player to load, so I never saw a frame of it. **You've looked at actual footage and I haven't — trust your read over my inference.**

Before abandoning it, though, check which standard you're applying.

### The bar is lower than you think

You're a film student. Your instinct is to judge footage against production standards — lighting, framing, sound. That's the wrong yardstick here.

For a vertical social clip the bar is: **legible face, audible voice, one good sentence.** That's it.

Self-recorded phone video is *native* to Reels and TikTok. Polished corporate video reads as an ad and underperforms. A 2022 filmmaker talking into their laptop in a kitchen is not a compromise on that platform — it's the format working correctly.

If you're rejecting clips because they look amateur, reconsider. If you're rejecting them because the audio is unintelligible or the face is unreadable, that's real — move on.

## If there's genuinely nothing: change the play

Don't force a bad clip. A weak spec asset is worse than none — it's a demonstration that you can't tell good from bad.

### Ask for footage instead of guessing

> Hi Ben,
>
> I'm Ima Kennerly — I won Future Filmmakers a few years back, and I'm at Newhouse now studying Television, Radio and Film.
>
> I'd like to cut you a sample: a panel or Q&A clip turned into a captioned vertical asset for Reels, free, no strings. What's on YouTube is mostly from 2020–22 — is there anything more recent I could work from? Happy to work with whatever you have.
>
> Ima Kennerly

It's a small ask, and it buys you three things a cold clip doesn't: it opens a conversation, it gets you better raw material, and it answers **the single most important question about this deal** — do they record their festival at all? — seven weeks before you'd otherwise find out.

### The thing worth noticing

If Portland Film Festival has almost no footage archive, that's not just a spec-asset problem. **It's a fit problem.** A media operations retainer edits footage the client produces. A festival that records almost nothing has nothing for you to operate on, no matter how warm the relationship or how dormant their Instagram.

Better to learn that from one email in August than from a signed pilot in September.

Which is an argument for running **Desert Island Studios and North East Productions in parallel right now**, not as backups. They're production companies — footage is what they make, so the raw material is guaranteed to exist. Their gap isn't capability, it's that production companies are notoriously terrible at marketing themselves. You have easy access to their published work for spec assets, and they're businesses with revenue rather than a volunteer-run nonprofit.

PFF has the better story. They may not have the better fit.
