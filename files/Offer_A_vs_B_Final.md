# Offer A vs Offer B — with everything we now know

I picked B in the first ten minutes and dismissed A on an assumption. The assumption was wrong. Here's the honest re-run.

---

## What killed Offer B

Not demand. **Supply.**

The offer promises 16 short-form vertical assets a month from a client's existing long-form video. That requires talking-head footage. Across five verified prospects — Portland Film Festival, HR Cloud, Nakisa, Topco, Cerbo — the footage was either missing or entirely screen recording.

That isn't bad luck. **B2B long-form video is screen recording.** Slides and a voiceover, maybe a small webcam box. Even a Deloitte-branded "fireside chat" turned out to be slides.

**Can it be salvaged?** Partly. You could change the deliverable to suit the footage that actually exists — carousels, quote cards, audiograms, captioned slide clips. But that's a weaker product, far more commoditized, and the AI tools you'd want to lean on are worst at exactly that source material. Salvageable, diminished.

---

## Why I was wrong about Offer A

I said local production studios and podcast hubs are cash-poor, so 20% of a small sponsorship isn't worth the work.

The cash-poor part is true. **The mistake was treating your doc's example as the boundary.** "Local production studios and podcast hubs" is one illustration of the client type. The same service sells to film festivals, regional conferences, arts nonprofits, sports and youth programs, membership organisations, and university programs — organisations with a real audience and unsophisticated sponsorship sales.

Portland Film Festival has Comcast and Koerner Camera as sponsors. Topco Media has Google, Meta, BMW, and Standard Bank. Sponsorship money at this level is real; the packaging usually isn't.

### Your pricing checks out

Industry standard for sponsorship sales consulting is a **retainer plus 10–20% commission on gross sponsorship revenue** — customarily 10–15% when a meaningful retainer is paid, sometimes laddered (15% on the first tranche, 10% above).

Your `$1,000 setup + 20%` is consistent with that. The 20% is at the top of the range, which is defensible precisely *because* $1,000 is a small setup fee rather than a real retainer.

Sponsorship deck work itself runs **$500–5,000** at the professional end (ignore the $10 Fiverr tier — that's a different market). And **44% of companies increased corporate sponsorship budgets** versus 2022, so the environment is favourable.

## The reframe that makes A actually work

**Sell the materials. Treat the commission as a lottery ticket.**

This is the one structural change I'd make to your doc. Sponsorship cycles run three to six months, and plenty of clients never close anything. If your income depends on their sales performance, you can do excellent work and earn nothing.

More practically: a twenty-year-old can credibly produce sponsorship *materials*. Guaranteeing sponsorship *outcomes* requires standing you don't have yet. Sell what you can obviously deliver.

> ### Sponsorship Kit — $1,200
> - Sponsorship deck with tiered packages
> - Audience and reach one-pager with real numbers
> - Tier structure and pricing rationale
> - Corporate membership framework
> - A prospect list of 25 target sponsors with named contacts
>
> **Optional:** 15% commission on anything closed within 12 months from the provided list.

Two projects a month is **$2,400** at roughly 12 hours each — inside 10 hrs/week, with commission as pure upside on top.

The 25-name prospect list is what makes this worth $1,200 rather than $400. Anyone can design a deck. Knowing *who to send it to* is the part that's actually hard, and it's the same research muscle you've been using all week.

**The timing signal:** organisations sell next year's sponsorship in the autumn. That means **September through November is the season**, which starts in four weeks.

## The honest risks

**Sales cycles are long.** Even selling the kit, decision-makers at nonprofits and conferences move slowly. Expect 6–10 weeks from first contact to payment.

**Credibility gap.** "I'll build your sponsorship materials" is defensible at twenty. "I'll run your sponsorship strategy" isn't yet. Stay on the materials side until you have two case studies.

**You'll need to learn tier design properly.** Sponsorship packaging has real conventions — inventory, valuation, activation rights. A weekend of study, not a semester, but don't wing it.

**Commission is genuinely unreliable.** Price so you're fine if it never arrives.

---

## Verdict

**Run Offer A.** It clears the filter that five failures taught us, it's on the direct path to the agency internships in your tracker, and your blueprint's pricing was sound — I just aimed it at the wrong client list.

**Retire Offer B.** Not because nobody wants it, but because the raw material it depends on mostly doesn't exist in usable form, and you've now proven that five times.

**Test before building, same as always:** offer one free sponsorship kit to a campus organisation or a Syracuse arts nonprofit. Two weeks, one case study, and you'll know whether the work is what you think it is before you charge anyone.
