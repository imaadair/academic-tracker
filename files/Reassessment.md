# Reassessment

Five dead ends. Worth understanding why before picking a sixth thing.

---

## The diagnosis

Every failure was **supply-side**. Not one person told you they didn't want the service.

| Attempt | What killed it |
|---|---|
| Portland Film Festival | No usable footage. No budget. |
| Desert Island / North East | Sell content services themselves |
| HR Cloud | Webinar was entirely screen recording |
| Topco Media | Ad-services company, in-house team |
| Nakisa | Also entirely screen recording |

The through-line: **the business depended on raw material you don't control and can't verify without an hour of digging per prospect.**

That's a structurally fragile design. Better targeting wouldn't have fixed it — I'd have kept finding screen recordings, because that's what B2B webinars *are*. I picked the source category without checking what the footage actually looked like, then discovered it one prospect at a time. That's on me.

---

## The filter for whatever comes next

1. **The input must be guaranteed.** Either it's your own knowledge, or it's something the client has by definition — not something you have to go verify exists.
2. **Qualification must be cheap.** If it takes an hour per prospect to find out whether a deal is even possible, the model doesn't survive a 5-hour week.
3. **Ideally, the hours count twice** — toward money *and* toward the Summer 2027 applications.

Point 1 is the one that was missing. A script always exists. Footage might not.

---

## The recommendation: script coverage, with a market lens

You already do this. Your résumé says it: *"Conducted script coverage, structural analysis, and commercial marketability evaluations for two feature screenplays."* You have Final Draft, Studio System, and IMDbPro. You've sat in a writers' room with Guinevere Turner and integrated executive-level notes.

### Why it clears every filter

**The supply problem disappears entirely.** The input is a screenplay. Writers have screenplays — that's the entire premise of a writer. There is no version of this where you spend an hour qualifying a prospect and discover the raw material doesn't exist.

**Qualification is instant.** Someone either wants coverage or doesn't. One email answers it.

**The hours count twice, and this is the real argument.** *"Read and evaluated 200+ screenplays"* is not a side-hustle line. It is **the** qualification for a development internship. A24, Blumhouse, Lionsgate, and Anonymous Content all hire development interns to do exactly this. Every hour you bill is an hour of the precise experience those applications are screening for.

Nothing else we've considered does that. The media ops business would have paid better per hour and done nothing for the applications sitting in your tracker.

### The differentiation that matters

Most freelance coverage is craft notes — *here's how to fix act two*. That's crowded and increasingly automated; there are sub-$25 AI coverage tools now.

**Your angle is the market read:** who buys this, what it comps to, which buyer's gap it fills, what's the realistic path. That's what your Development Analyst work already was, and it's what almost no freelance reader can do because it requires knowing the business, not just the craft.

It's also — not coincidentally — exactly what a development executive does.

## The market, honestly

**Rates:**

| Type | Rate | Time | Effective |
|---|---|---|---|
| Competition/contest reader | $25–60/script | ~2 hrs | $15–30/hr |
| Direct-to-writer coverage | $75–200/script | ~2 hrs | $40–100/hr |
| Studio story analyst (union) | $1,544–1,857/week | — | not accessible yet |

Skip contest reading — the rate is bad. **Direct-to-writer is the business.**

**A market disruption just opened a door.** Coverfly shut down in August 2025, and its parent company also closed ScreenCraft, WeScreenplay, and The Script Lab. The main aggregator ecosystem for screenwriter feedback collapsed within a year. Thousands of writers who used those services are now looking for somewhere to go. The Black List is still running, but the field thinned out dramatically.

That's a genuine opening, and it's recent enough that it hasn't been filled.

**The math:** 12–16 scripts a month at $125 is **$1,500–2,000**, at roughly 2 hours each. That's 24–32 hours — right at your ceiling, with little room for sales. Realistically: $1,200–1,500/month by month four, $2,000 once you have repeat clients and referrals.

Same order of magnitude as everything else we've looked at. The difference is that this one also builds the thing you actually want.

## The honest weaknesses

**Finding writers is still a sales problem.** It's the one thing that doesn't get easier. But writers cluster visibly — screenwriting subreddits, Twitter/Bluesky, Discord servers, film school programs, Stage 32, contest-adjacent communities. They're findable in a way that "companies with talking-head webinar footage" was not.

**AI coverage tools exist and are cheap.** Sub-$25 services will take the low end. Your defense is the market read — an AI can summarize a script but can't tell a writer which of Blumhouse's gaps their horror script fills. Price above the AI tier deliberately; don't compete on cost.

**Reading is slow.** Two hours a script is real, and it's cognitively heavy work to do after a full course load. Ten a month is a realistic ceiling during term.

**One conflict to watch:** if you're reading for a production company and also selling coverage to writers, be careful not to blur the two. Keep them separate.

## If you want money faster

**Film school admissions consulting** pays materially better — college consultants charge $100–300/hour, and almost none can advise on a creative supplement, portfolio, or director's reel.

You got into Newhouse, did USC's program and NYU Paris, and won the Portland Film Festival Future Filmmakers competition as a teenager. For a 17-year-old applying to USC, Tisch, Chapman, or Newhouse, that's an unusually credible advisor.

Application season is **now through January**, which overlaps your winter break. At $100/hour and 8 hours a week, that's $3,200/month during season.

It does nothing for your development applications. But it's the fastest honest path to cash if that's the priority, and it's seasonal enough not to compete with coverage year-round.

---

## What I'd do

**Script coverage as the business.** It's the only option where the money and the Summer 2027 applications pull in the same direction, and the supply problem that killed five attempts simply doesn't exist.

**Admissions consulting as a seasonal add-on** if you want cash faster — it runs Nov–Jan and then stops.

Before building anything this time: **test demand with five conversations.** Post in one screenwriting community offering two free coverages in exchange for feedback and a testimonial. If nobody bites, we've learned that in a week rather than five weeks.

That's the step I skipped last time.
